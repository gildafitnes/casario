Why this matters?

There was a period, not particularly distant, when living in the centre of Madrid did not necessarily require dedicating most of one's income to housing. Lavapiés, Malasaña and Tribunal were already dense, imperfect and constantly changing neighbourhoods, but the relatively low cost of occupying space created a particular economic condition: it allowed people with very little capital to remain in the city.

Artists lived alongside migrants, students, musicians, craftspeople, shopkeepers and people whose projects did not yet have — and sometimes never acquired — any obvious economic value. This mixture is often described afterwards as a cultural phenomenon, as if a neighbourhood simply became interesting because a certain generation happened to live there. But culture also has a material infrastructure. It needs rooms, workshops, rehearsal spaces, bars and bookshops; more importantly, it needs enough disposable time for activities whose economic return cannot be calculated in advance.

In 2011, one of the people behind Casa Río rented a room on Calle Palma, in Malasaña, for €100 a month. Spain's official minimum monthly wage that year was €641.40. (1) The figure is a personal recollection rather than a statistical description of the neighbourhood, but the proportion is useful: that room represented roughly sixteen per cent of a minimum monthly salary.

A low housing cost does something more significant than simply leaving additional money in a bank account. It changes the amount of risk a person can afford.

Someone whose basic expenses are relatively small can reduce their working hours, rehearse for months, attempt to open a shop, publish something nobody has commissioned, organise a concert for thirty people or simply spend time with other people without transforming that encounter into an economic transaction. Most of these activities are financially insignificant when they occur. Their accumulation, however, is often what later becomes described as the identity, atmosphere or cultural value of a neighbourhood.

There is a paradox here. Many of the activities that eventually make an area desirable are possible precisely because the area is inexpensive. Once that desirability becomes economically measurable, however, part of the value produced collectively over decades begins to appear in the private price of land and property.

Madrid offers a particularly clear example of this process. Historical municipal data show that the average appraisal value of used housing in the city increased from €1,481 per square metre in the first quarter of 2000 to €3,063 in the second quarter of 2004. (2) In slightly more than four years, the figure more than doubled.

The financial crisis interrupted this trajectory. Residential rents in Madrid declined between approximately 2011 and 2014, before entering another sustained period of growth from the middle of the decade. (3) The transformation of the city therefore cannot be explained solely through Airbnb, tourism, digital nomads or foreign investment. Much of it predates these phenomena.

What changed more fundamentally was the economic role assigned to urban property.

A home is a place where somebody lives, but it can simultaneously function as an investment, a source of rental income, collateral for acquiring further assets and an object expected to appreciate over time. These functions coexist within exactly the same physical space, although their objectives are not necessarily compatible.

For the resident, the usefulness of an apartment is relatively straightforward: stability, proximity, a place to sleep, cook, store belongings and construct a life. For an investor, the same apartment can be evaluated through rent, appreciation, liquidity and expected return.

The object is identical. The logic applied to it is different.

This distinction matters because housing is not an ordinary commodity. It is possible to postpone buying clothes, technology or furniture. It is considerably harder to withdraw from the market for physical space. Everybody requires somewhere to exist.

For that reason, differences in purchasing power have unusually direct consequences.

Madrid has also become more deeply integrated into international flows of tourism, capital and labour. Someone earning a salary in London, Amsterdam, Berlin or New York may arrive in Madrid looking for what generations of migrants have always looked for: somewhere where life appears more possible.

There is nothing inherently problematic about that movement.

The tension emerges when people with radically different purchasing power compete for the same finite resource. If one household can comfortably pay €1,600 per month while another can pay €800, the housing market does not evaluate their histories, their relationship to the neighbourhood or the social value of their presence. It registers demand and purchasing power.

No conspiracy is necessary. Thousands of individually rational economic decisions can produce a structural transformation.

Owners are encouraged to seek the highest sustainable rent. Short-term accommodation may generate greater returns than residential tenancy. Buildings may acquire greater financial value as investment vehicles than through their everyday use. Large financial actors can also purchase property at a scale impossible for individual residents to reproduce.

Madrid provides an unusually concrete example. In 2013, the municipal housing company sold 1,860 publicly owned rental homes distributed across eighteen developments to Fidere, a company associated with Blackstone. (6) The importance of the case is not that it explains Madrid's housing crisis on its own — it does not — but that it reveals how residential space can migrate from one economic logic to another.

The effects eventually become visible at street level.

The buildings remain. The names of the streets remain. The metro stations remain in exactly the same places. What changes is the network of relationships taking place between them.

Workshops disappear because their activity cannot support the rent. Small shops close or change their business model. Informal gathering places become economically difficult to maintain. Residents who have spent ten or twenty years in an area begin to recognise fewer people around them.

Madrid City Council has itself documented these processes in Distrito Centro, examining relationships between tourist accommodation, housing affordability, displacement of residents and transformations in traditional commerce, including Malasaña. (4)

The scale of tourist accommodation is particularly significant in the centre. Municipal data have calculated 8,363 tourist-use dwellings against a residential rental stock of 26,070 units in Distrito Centro — the equivalent of approximately 32% of the conventional rental stock. (5) These categories are not perfectly interchangeable, and the figure should not be read as meaning that precisely one third of homes have disappeared from the residential market. It does, however, indicate the magnitude of the competing economic use of residential space in the district.

What disappears through these processes is not some pure or authentic version of Madrid.

Cities have never been static. People arrive and leave. Languages, businesses and customs change. Lavapiés, Malasaña and Tribunal were themselves produced through migration, conflict, commerce, cultural exchange and continuous transformation.

Nostalgia is therefore not particularly useful here.

The more interesting question concerns the material conditions that allow collective culture to emerge.

A city needs places where activity does not have to justify itself immediately through profitability: somewhere to rehearse, organise a reading, repair something, learn from somebody else, begin an uncertain project or simply remain for several hours without being required to continuously consume.

These places form a kind of social infrastructure. They are rarely spectacular individually. A workshop, a rehearsal room, a neighbourhood bar or a small cultural space may appear economically marginal. Together, however, they create much of what makes urban life different from simply occupying residential units near places of employment.

And all of them require the same thing: space.

As the price of that space increases, the threshold for participating in the city also rises. Stable income becomes more important. Experimentation becomes more expensive. Failure becomes harder to absorb. Activities capable of generating high revenues per square metre progressively acquire an advantage over activities whose value is cultural, social or difficult to quantify.

This also produces a longer-term economic effect.

Property ownership makes further accumulation of property easier. An existing asset can appreciate, generate rent or be used as collateral. Someone without property occupies the opposite position: an increasing proportion of income may be transferred toward obtaining temporary access to an asset owned by somebody else.

Capital facilitates access to property. Property can generate additional capital. Rent, meanwhile, makes the accumulation of the initial capital required for ownership more difficult.

The process is not absolute and its future cannot be predicted mechanically. But it raises a relatively simple question: what happens to a city when an increasing proportion of the people who constitute its everyday life no longer control the spaces in which that life occurs?

Casa Río begins with that question.

It does not attempt to recover a fictional Madrid of the past, nor to preserve a neighbourhood as though it were a museum. The objective is considerably smaller and, for that reason, perhaps more practical: to remove one physical space from this cycle.

To acquire a place whose value does not need to be measured primarily through the maximum rent it can produce or the price for which it may later be sold.

A place whose value can instead accumulate through use: through the projects developed there, the people who meet there, the knowledge exchanged there and the culture produced there.

One space, collectively acquired and protected from speculation.

Not as a solution to the housing market.

As an experiment in what becomes possible when a small piece of the city is no longer required to behave like an investment.
